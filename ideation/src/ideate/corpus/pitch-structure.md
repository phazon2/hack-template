# Pitch structure for a three-minute hackathon presentation

Most hackathon pitches are three to five minutes with a couple of minutes of
questions. That is not enough time to explain a project; it is enough time to show
one thing and make the judge want to ask about the rest. Structure the pitch around
that.

## The shape

Thirty seconds of who and what hurts. Sixty to ninety seconds of demo, with the demo
moment landing by the ninety-second mark of the whole pitch. Thirty seconds on what
makes it novel and how it works. Fifteen seconds on what you would do next. Stop with
time to spare. Judges reward brevity and penalise overruns, and a pitch that finishes
early leaves room for the questions that let you show depth.

## Open with the user, not the market

The first sentence names a person in a situation: a role, a moment, a pain. "A
volunteer coordinator at a food bank, at 6am, matching drivers to routes by phone." Do
not open with the size of the industry, the number of people affected, or a definition
of the problem space. Judges have heard those openings all day and they signal a team
that has not talked to a user.

## Demo before explanation

Show the product doing the thing before explaining how it works. The explanation lands
better once the judge has seen the behaviour, and if the demo fails at least the judge
saw you try. A demo-first pitch also forces the team to have a demo, which is the
point. Narrate the demo in the user's voice: "she pastes the route list, and…" rather
than "the backend calls the geocoding API and…".

## The one-sentence key innovation

After the demo, say what is new in one sentence. If it takes more than one sentence,
the innovation is not sharp enough yet. Frame it against the closest existing thing:
"Unlike X, which does A, this does B, which matters because C." Naming the closest
existing product does not hurt you; it shows you know the space and it pre-empts the
judge's most likely question.

## How it works, in one breath

One sentence on the stack, one on the hardest technical problem you solved, one on the
data source. Judges scoring feasibility want to hear that the team understood the hard
part; they do not want a component diagram. If a sponsor's API is central, name it
here and say what it made possible.

## Honesty about what is seeded

If part of the demo uses seeded data or a recorded segment, say so in half a sentence
and move on. Judges notice fakery and punish it; they do not punish honesty about scope.
"The route data is seeded; the matching is live" is a strong sentence, not a weak one.

## Close on the next step, not the vision

End with one concrete thing you would build next, ideally something a judge might have
wondered about during the demo. Avoid the "in the future this could become a platform"
ending; it is the most common ending in the room and it dilutes what you actually
showed.

## Handling questions

Expect three questions: how is this different from X, what happens when Y goes wrong,
and could this scale. Prepare a two-sentence answer to each. When you do not know, say
so and say what you would try. Questions are a chance to show the depth the pitch did
not have time for, so have the second-best feature ready to show if asked.

## Slides, if any

At most three slides: a title with the public URL or QR code, one slide with the named
user and pain, and one with the key innovation and closest existing. No architecture
slides, no team slides, no bullet lists of features. The demo is the deck.

## Who presents

The person most comfortable speaking presents; the person most comfortable with the
product drives the demo. Rehearse them together at least three times. The presenter
should never touch the keyboard during the demo and the driver should never speak
unless asked. Two voices on a three-minute pitch confuse the room.

## Timing discipline

Rehearse against a timer with a hard stop. Cut until the pitch lands with twenty
seconds of margin. If the demo can run long because of latency, decide in advance what
to skip. A pitch cut off mid-sentence by the timer loses more than the missing content;
it signals a team that could not scope, and scoping is what the judges are assessing.

## A pitch skeleton to fill in

Who: a named role in a named situation. Pain: the specific thing that goes wrong. Demo:
the moment on screen. New: unlike the closest thing, this does what. How: stack, hard
part, data. Honest: what is seeded. Next: one concrete step. Fill each blank with one
sentence and the pitch is written.
