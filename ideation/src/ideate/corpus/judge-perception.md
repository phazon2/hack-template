# How judges perceive projects, and how to be perceived well

Every rubric assumes a judge who evaluates your project on its merits. The actual judge is a
tired human who has watched many submissions in a few hours, cannot verify almost anything you
claim, and will shortly argue with other judges about who wins. That is the real evaluation
environment, and building for it is as legitimate as building for the browser your users run.

This is competitive presentation craft — the discipline a startup uses to raise money. The work
still has to be real; the point is making real work land, because work that lands beats
equivalent work that does not.

## The judge cannot verify, so they substitute

A judge has no time to read your code and no way to test your claims. Faced with a question they
cannot answer — *is this technically impressive?* — people substitute one they can: *does this
look impressive?* The substitution is automatic, and it is how anyone evaluates under time
pressure.

The practical consequence is that visible signals of depth carry real weight. A live map with
points resolving on it, a physical device, streaming video, a rendered simulation, a visible
equation: these communicate difficulty before you say a word. Two projects of equal real
difficulty do not score equally if one of them shows its difficulty and the other hides it behind
a text box.

## The halo effect is one lever with many outputs

One strong salient impression colours judgement of every other attribute, including attributes
the observer has no evidence about. A project with a confident presenter and a polished interface
gets read as more technically sound, more complete and more useful — not because anyone reasoned
their way there, but because a positive first impression propagates.

So interface polish is not superficial here: it is the cheapest multiplier on perceived
technical quality. Spend the hour on what the judge will look at, not the abstraction only you
will see. The same applies to delivery — rehearsed, calm and specific reads as competent, and
perceived competence transfers onto the artifact.

## Make it easy to process, because easy reads as true

Claims that are easy to understand are judged more favourably and believed more readily than
claims that require effort — a well-documented effect of processing fluency. Under decision
fatigue this gets stronger, not weaker.

So: one idea in plain words, repeated. A name that says what the thing does. A screen the judge
can absorb without narration. If explaining your project needs an architecture diagram, you have
lost the room. The architecture can be excellent and the explanation still simple; those are
independent choices, and most teams get the second one wrong.

## They remember the peak and the ending

People do not remember an average of an experience. They remember its most intense moment and how
it ended. A demo with one genuinely striking moment and a clean close will be recalled better
than a uniformly good demo that trails off while you look for the right tab.

Design the peak deliberately — the moment that makes someone lean in — and put it early enough
to survive an interruption. Then end on purpose, with a sentence you chose, rather than because
time ran out.

## Someone has to argue for you in the room

This is the most underused lever and the one almost nobody plans for. When judges convene, your
project does not defend itself. A judge who liked it has to describe it to colleagues who may not
have seen it, and win an argument against other advocates.

That judge needs ammunition: a sentence they can repeat accurately from memory after seeing you
once, hours ago, among many. If your project can be summarised in one concrete sentence — who it
is for, what it does, why it is hard — it can be advocated for. If it cannot, it dies quietly in
deliberation no matter how good it was, because your advocate stumbles and someone else's does
not.

Write that sentence before you build. Say it in the demo, verbatim, more than once. Put it on the
submission page as the first line. You are not writing it for the judge who watched you; you are
writing it for the argument they will have without you in the room.

## You are judged against your neighbours, not against an ideal

Standards drift across a judging session, and each project is anchored by what came immediately
before it. Where the format is explicitly comparative — see `judging-formats.md` — this is the
whole mechanism, but it operates under rubrics too.

Two consequences. First, distinctiveness beats incremental quality: being the only project that
does something unusual is worth more than being ten percent better at something six teams also
did. Second, if you can influence when you present, later is often better than early, because
early projects set the anchor and later ones are measured against a standard the judge has
already formed.

## Framing decides which comparison is made

The same artifact can be presented as a small tool or as the first working piece of something
larger, and those framings land differently even though the demo is identical. Framing is not
spin; it is choosing which reference class you are evaluated in. A judge deciding "is this a good
utility?" applies different standards than one deciding "is this an early version of something
important?"

Choose the frame that makes your real strengths load-bearing, and state it explicitly rather than
hoping the judge infers it. If your strength is technical difficulty, frame around what was hard.
If it is a real user with a real problem, frame around the user and let the technology recede.

## Where this stops being persuasion

Everything above works on how truthful information is presented, sequenced and framed. The line
is claiming something that is not so, and it is worth knowing exactly where it sits, because
crossing it loses prizes after they have been awarded rather than before.

Do not present a recording as a live run. Do not describe a feature as working when it is stubbed;
say "this part is seeded" and move on, since judges rarely punish a stated simplification and
always punish a discovered one. Do not misstate what you built during the event when the rules
constrain that, and do not overstate who did what. If a judge asks a direct question, answer it
straight — a confident honest answer about a limitation reads as competence, and a dodge that
gets caught destroys the halo you spent the whole demo building.

Everything on the near side of that line is yours to use, and most teams use almost none of it.

## What to do with this

Before building: write the one sentence your advocate will repeat, and choose the frame. While
building: put the hour into what the judge will see. Before presenting: design the peak, choose
the ending, rehearse until delivery is calm. In the room: lead with the visible thing, state the
sentence, be specific about limitations when asked.
